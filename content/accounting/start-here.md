You need no accounting at all to start. This page explains the few ideas behind every bookkeeping entry, using a lemonade stand. Afterwards, the [flash card game](/accounting/flashcards/) lets you practise on the same stand, one sale at a time.

## 1. Everything the business has, someone has a claim on

Picture Mia's lemonade stand. In its books, the stand counts as someone of its own, separate from Mia. At any moment it **has** things: cash in the tin, lemons and cups, a table. Those things are held in the stand's name: the stand has the **title** to them. Every one of them came from somewhere, so someone has a claim on it. Either the stand **owes** someone for it, such as a loan from Mom, or the claim is Mia's: her **stake** in the stand.

Mia's stake is not ownership of the stand's things. Once she puts $20 of her savings into the tin, that $20 is the stand's, not hers, and she cannot own what she has no title to. What Mia holds instead is a claim on the stand: to whatever is left once the stand has paid everything it owes. That claim is called **equity**, and Mia is the stand's **equity holder**. Books often call her the "owner", but that word misleads: she has a claim on the cash, the lemons and the table, not title to them.

So the two sides always match:

> **What the stand has = What it owes + Mia's stake**

Accountants call these **assets**, **liabilities** and **equity**: *Assets = Liabilities + Equity*. This is the whole of accounting in one line. Everything else is keeping it true.

## 2. Every event changes at least two things

Nothing happens to a business on its own. When Mia puts $20 of her savings into the tin, two things change. The stand has $20 more cash, and Mia's stake in the stand is $20 bigger. Both sides of the equation went up by $20, so it still balances.

When Mia then spends $15 of the stand's cash on lemons for the stand, two things change again. Cash goes down $15, and lemons, which the stand also has, go up $15. One side of the equation shifted, but its total didn't change.

That is why it is called **double-entry** bookkeeping: every event is written down twice, once for each thing that changed.

## 3. Debit means left. Credit means right.

Bookkeepers keep each account, such as Cash, as a **T**: a line across the top with the account's name, and a line down the middle. Amounts written on the **left** side are **debits**. Amounts written on the **right** side are **credits**.

| Cash: debit (left) | Cash: credit (right) |
|---|---|
| $20 Mia puts in her savings | $15 lemons bought |
| **Balance: $5** | |

"Debit" and "credit" don't mean good or bad, or money in and money out for you. They only mean left and right.

## 4. The one rule

Which side a change goes on depends on what kind of account it is:

| Goes **up** on the **left** (debit) | Goes **up** on the **right** (credit) |
|---|---|
| **Assets**: what the business has (cash, lemons, the table, money customers owe it) | **Liabilities**: what the business owes (a loan, a bill not yet paid) |
| **Expenses**: what running it costs (paying a helper, lemons used up) | **Equity**: the equity holder's claim (money Mia put in) |
| | **Income**: what it earns (sales) |

To make an account go **down**, use the other side.

Income and expenses sit where they do because they change Mia's stake. A sale makes Mia's stake bigger, so income goes up on the stake's side, the right. A cost makes her stake smaller, and the stake goes down on the left, so expenses go up on the left.

In every entry the debits and the credits add up to the same amount. That keeps the equation balanced.

## 5. How to work out any entry

Ask three questions:

1. **What two things changed?** (Cash, and Mia's stake.)
2. **What kind is each?** (Cash is something the stand has. Mia's stake is equity.)
3. **Did each go up or down?** (Both went up.)

Then the rule gives the side. Cash is an asset going up, so it's on the left: **debit Cash**. Mia's stake is equity going up, so it's on the right: **credit Mia's Money**.
