## About Russell Rogers Smith

Russell Rogers Smith was born on 16 February 1884 at Fort Qu'Appelle, in the District of Assiniboia of the North-West Territories, now Saskatchewan.[^about] His father, Richard James Smith, built wooden bridges for the Canadian Pacific Railway. He and three of his crew persuaded the railway's surveyor, Dewdney, to survey homesteads for them in the Qu'Appelle valley, which the book calls the first homesteads surveyed in what became Saskatchewan.[^about]

The book says that because his parents fed the Cree when they were starving, his father was made a blood brother of the Crees, and Smith himself was later made a chief of the tribe: Chief Wapanatak, "Morning Star" in Cree.[^about] A letter of 6 November 1947, reprinted in the book, has Smith thanking C. Fraser Elliott for his congratulations on becoming a Chief of the Crees.[^letters] These are Smith's own account and his editor's; we have found no independent record of either honour.

Smith became a mechanical engineer and, as a hobby, a student of international and constitutional law. The book credits him with drafting the resolution Mackenzie King presented to the Imperial Conference of 1926, which it calls the basis of the Statute of Westminster, 1931.[^about] In 1947 he was living at Black Bear Creek, near Likely in British Columbia's Cariboo District, and writing to Elliott about his income tax.[^letters] In 1946 George Barr, K.C., was sent by Mackenzie King to Montreal to examine him; the transcript survives.[^mythsmith]

His other writings are *Alberta Has The Sovereign Right To Issue And Use Its Own Credit* (1937), *Inside Canada* (1939) and an unpublished manuscript, *The Sword of Damocles*.[^mythsmith] *Ho, Canada!* was published in New York by Vantage Press in 1965.[^psu] His final chapter proposes a "do-it-yourself" way for an individual Canadian to assert the right to vote as a Canadian.

The 1996 edition, re-edited by Jean-Paul Rhéaume of Sainte-Foy, Quebec, adds the editor's own notes in brackets.[^about] Our copy of the PDF, and of each of the documents below, was downloaded from The Myth is Canada, which collected them.
