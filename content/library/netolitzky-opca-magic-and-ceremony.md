Donald J. Netolitzky is complex litigant management counsel at the Alberta Court of Queen's Bench, with a doctorate in microbiology and a law degree. He writes in his own name, not the court's. The article doesn't mention Scott Duncan or The Tender For Law.

### What pseudolaw is

Netolitzky's word for it is **pseudolaw**: rules that copy the look and language of law, but don't come from any source a court recognises. Its users claim special authority, or immunity from the state. In the United States it grew among the Posse Comitatus, tax protesters, militias and the Sovereign Citizens. After 2000 it spread to Moorish groups and to Canada's Freemen-on-the-Land, then across the Commonwealth and into Europe, with its content oddly unchanged.

In 2012, Associate Chief Justice Rooke's judgment in *Meads v. Meads* reviewed about 150 decisions and gave the whole family one name: **Organized Pseudolegal Commercial Arguments (OPCA)**. In Canada, OPCA arguments mostly turn up in four kinds of case:

- avoiding income tax;
- getting out of debts, or paying them with "money for nothing";
- getting out of criminal charges;
- suing officials and others over spurious claims.

Netolitzky splits pseudolaw in two:

- **Legal error.** A real rule, misread. His main example is the claim that silence means consent: send a notice, a fee schedule or a set of letters, and if nobody objects, it binds them. Courts answer that a contract can't be forced on someone by their silence. This kind can be argued with, point by point.
- **"Otherlaw".** Ideas with no root in law, history or logic at all, which he compares to magic: rituals meant to compel a court. He gives three kinds:
  - *Sympathetic magic:* a homemade "order" from the International Criminal Court, stamped and signed, as if a copy of a thing carried its power.
  - *Cargo cult law:* imitating the look of legal power without knowing what it does. His example is postage stamps on court papers, which different gurus explain in different, incompatible ways.
  - *The "Strawman":* the idea that each person has a second, legal self, named in capital letters and tied to them by their birth papers, that can be cut away. He finds no precedent for it at all.

He finds that belief is uneven. Some people use these schemes only for what they hope to get out of them; others believe the story behind them. Pseudolaw doesn't build its communities: it takes hold in existing ones that already see government as illegitimate. He ends by calling some of it ritual rather than law, and asking courts to look to psychologists, anthropologists and sociologists to understand it. He names the Quatloos forum among the sources of "effective legal and historical criticism" of pseudolaw.

### Why we use accounting to see surety

This part is The Tender For Law's own teaching, in the words of its articles and [dictionary](/dictionary/).

**Surety is the responsible party.** Scott Duncan defines [surety](/dictionary/#surety) as the one who answers for an obligation: surety equals liability ([What Is the Difference Between Legal and Lawful](/articles/scott-duncan/what-is-the-diffrence-between-legal-and-lawful/); [Surety for Idiots](/articles/scott-duncan/surety-for-idiots/)). The group's working definition of LEGAL is "it's all surety and accounting".

**You can't see a liability; you can only see it written down.** Who answers for a debt is a promise, not a thing; Scott calls money "debt in transit" ([dictionary](/dictionary/#money)). It shows only where it is recorded. That record is accounting. Chris Schulte puts it in one line: accounting is the mechanism by which the story of the transfer of surety is told ([An Article About Accounting](/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/)).

**The books show who holds it.** In double-entry bookkeeping every entry has two sides: one party's debit is the other's credit, so every transaction shows where an obligation came from and where it went. Everything a business has, someone has a claim on ([Start Here: Debits and Credits](/accounting/start-here/)). Those claims are either liabilities, which the business answers for, or equity, the owner's stake. Both carry a credit balance, and which one an entry is decides who is the responsible party.

**So you read the books to find the surety.** To find who is surety for something, follow the accounting: whose books carry it as a liability, and who holds the matching claim. That is why Ryan Wallace's advice, when you're stuck, is "LEGAL = SURETY & ACCOUNTING, and DO the damn ACCOUNTING" ([Show Me the Money](/articles/various-authors/show-me-the-money/)).
