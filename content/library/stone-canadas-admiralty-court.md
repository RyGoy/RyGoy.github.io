Arthur J. Stone was a judge of the Federal Court of Appeal in Ottawa. He gave this paper at Montreal on 16 June 2001, for the fiftieth anniversary of the Canadian Maritime Law Association.

Canada has had a national admiralty court since 1891. Under an imperial statute of 1890, the Colonial Courts of Admiralty Act, Parliament declared the Exchequer Court of Canada a "Colonial Court of Admiralty", replacing the unpopular British vice-admiralty courts after more than thirty years of debate. Stone follows the court through four parts:

- **Birth and development.** The early courts, a separate maritime court for Ontario, and the first national admiralty court.
- **Jurisdiction.** At first the court's jurisdiction followed England's. Only after the Statute of Westminster, 1931 could Canada set its own court's jurisdiction, and the Federal Court Act of 1971 widened it greatly, continuing the Exchequer Court as the Federal Court of Canada.
- **Sources of the law.** "Canadian maritime law", the law the court administers, traced back through modern statutes to the sea codes and ordinances of continental Europe and to Roman law.
- **Judicial reform.** How the courts have modernized old rules of maritime law where they had become unfair.

He ends with the Federal Court, under its wider jurisdiction and new rules, among the leading admiralty courts of the world.
