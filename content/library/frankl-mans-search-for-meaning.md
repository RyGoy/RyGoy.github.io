Viktor Frankl (1905–1997) was a Viennese psychiatrist and neurologist. In 1942 he was deported with his wife and parents to the Theresienstadt ghetto, and from there to Auschwitz and two subcamps of Dachau, where he was freed in April 1945. His father died at Theresienstadt, his mother and brother at Auschwitz, and his wife at Bergen-Belsen. He wrote this book in nine days the following year.

The first part tells life in the camps from the inside: the shock of arrival, the apathy that followed, and what came after liberation. Frankl watched which prisoners held on and which gave up, and concluded that what kept people alive was less strength or luck than a reason to go on: a person waiting for them, a work to finish, or the way they chose to bear what could not be changed. Everything can be taken from a man, he writes, except the freedom to choose his attitude to his circumstances.

The second part sets out logotherapy, the school of psychotherapy Frankl founded on that observation: that the primary drive in people is not pleasure or power but meaning, found in what we create, in what and whom we love, and in how we face suffering. Later editions add his 1984 postscript, "The Case for a Tragic Optimism."
