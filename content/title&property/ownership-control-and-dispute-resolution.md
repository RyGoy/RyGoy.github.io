## Most disputes are property disputes

Nearly every dispute comes down to one question: **who holds the better claim to this thing, and what may they do with it?** Courts settle contracts, debts, boundaries, inheritances and injuries by first deciding who owns what. A party that can prove ownership and control starts the dispute ahead. A party that cannot is arguing from someone else's ground.

Ownership is not fixed. Rights **move**: they are sold, leased, pledged, given, inherited, held in trust, divided over time, used up and taken away by courts. Every move leaves a record, and the main record is the **balance sheet**. To settle a dispute, you trace where the right went and find it on someone's books.

## The bundle of rights

The law treats ownership as a bundle of separate rights rather than one solid thing. The usual sticks in the bundle are:

| Right | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Possession** | To hold and occupy the property |
| **Use (control)** | To decide how the property is used |
| **Exclusion** | To keep others off or out |
| **Enjoyment** | To take its income, produce and benefit |
| **Disposition** | To sell, lease, mortgage, gift or bequeath it |

The sticks can be split up and handed to different people. A tenant holds possession and use, while the landlord keeps disposition. A lender holding a mortgage has a claim on the property, but the borrower keeps possession. When people argue over property, they are usually arguing over **which sticks each of them holds**. The first step in any dispute is to name the specific right at stake.

The **right to exclude** is often called the most important stick. Without it, the others cannot be protected. It never appears as a line on a balance sheet, but it is what makes every asset line worth anything: an asset that anyone could take is not an asset.

## The balance sheet: where rights are recorded

Accounting is the language that ownership is proved in. A balance sheet lists:

- **Assets:** the rights you hold.
- **Liabilities:** other people's claims against what you hold.
- **Equity:** what is left for the owner once those claims are paid. This is the owner's **residual claim**.

The equation always balances: *Assets = Liabilities + Equity.* Every claim on an asset belongs either to a creditor or to the owner.

Each kind of right has its own place:

| Right or interest | Where it sits on the holder's balance sheet |
|---|---|
| Full ownership of land, buildings or equipment | **Asset:** property, plant and equipment |
| Goods held for sale | **Asset:** inventory |
| The right to be paid (a loan, unpaid rent, a sale on credit) | **Asset:** a receivable, such as a note or mortgage receivable |
| The right to use someone else's property under a lease | **Asset:** a right-of-use asset, with a matching lease liability |
| Patents, copyrights, trademarks, licences, purchased easements | **Asset:** intangible assets |
| A creditor's claim on your property (a mortgage or lien) | **Liability:** mortgage or note payable. The property stays on your side, and the pledge is disclosed in the notes |
| The owner's residual claim | **Equity** |
| A disputed or uncertain claim | **Notes to the financial statements**, as a contingency. It becomes a liability only when a loss is probable and can be estimated |

Every transaction has a matching entry on both sides, so a right that leaves one balance sheet arrives on another. A sale, a loan or a lien that was never properly recorded is hard to prove later.

Public records do the same job for land: recorded deeds, mortgages, liens, surveys and tax records. In most places, the first party to **record** a valid interest takes priority over later claimants.

When records are complete and consistent, most disputes end quickly. When they are missing, a court has to reconstruct the history from testimony and circumstance, which is slow, costly and uncertain. Good records are the cheapest dispute resolution there is.

## How rights move

Each way of moving a right hands over certain sticks, and each has a recognisable trail on both parties' books:

| Transaction | Sticks that move | The party giving them up | The party receiving them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sale** | All of them | Asset removed; cash or a receivable added; a gain or loss equal to the price minus book value | Asset added at its cost; cash paid or a liability taken on |
| **Lease** | Possession and use, for a term | Usually keeps the property on its books and keeps depreciating it; records rent as income | Right-of-use asset and lease liability (for leases longer than 12 months) |
| **Mortgage or lien** | A security claim on the property | The borrower keeps the property and adds a mortgage payable | The lender adds a mortgage receivable |
| **Gift** | All of them | Asset removed; equity falls | Asset added; equity rises |
| **Inheritance** | All of them, at death | The estate distributes the asset | The heir records it, usually at its value at the date of death |
| **Trust** | Legal title to the trustee; the benefit to the beneficiary | The grantor removes the asset | The trust keeps its own books. The trustee does not count trust property as personal wealth. The beneficiary holds a beneficial interest |
| **Life estate and remainder** | Possession now to one person; possession later to another | The grantor removes the asset, or keeps a reversion | Each holder records an interest with its own value; together they equal the whole |
| **Foreclosure, judgment or adverse possession** | Whatever the court or the law awards | Asset written off, often as a loss | Asset added, or a receivable cancelled in exchange for the property |

Read the table from either side and the totals agree. That is why a complete set of books, on both sides of a transfer, is the strongest evidence of who holds what.

## Legal title and equitable (beneficial) ownership

- **Legal title** is the formal, recorded ownership: the name on the deed, registration or certificate of title.
- **Equitable or beneficial ownership** is the right to enjoy the property and its benefits, even when someone else holds legal title. A trust is the classic example: the trustee holds legal title, and the beneficiary holds the benefit.

These can belong to different people. In a dispute, a court asks who holds title, whether that title is good, and whether someone else holds an equitable interest that limits it. The **chain of title**, meaning the unbroken record of every transfer from one owner to the next, is usually the deciding evidence.

**On the balance sheet, accounting follows control and benefit, not just the name on the document.** Two examples:

- **A financed car.** The lender is listed as lienholder, and in some states it holds the title certificate. Even so, the buyer records the car as an asset and the loan as a liability, because the buyer has the use, the benefit and the risk.
- **A trust.** The trustee holds legal title but does not list trust property among their own assets.

When legal title and benefit point to different people, the balance sheet usually follows the benefit. When the two records disagree, that disagreement is often the dispute.

## Reversionary interests and remainders

Ownership can also be divided **over time**. A person can hold the property now (a **present interest**) while someone else holds the right to it later (a **future interest**). A future interest is a real property right *today*, even though possession comes later. It can be recorded, sold, inherited and defended in court.

**Reversionary interests** are future interests the grantor (the person who gave the property) **keeps for themselves**:

- **Reversion.** The owner grants less than everything they have. For example, "to Ann for her life" gives Ann a **life estate**. When Ann dies, the property automatically **reverts** to the grantor or the grantor's heirs.
- **Possibility of reverter.** The grant lasts only *so long as* a condition holds, for example "to the town *so long as* it is used as a park". When the use stops, ownership returns to the grantor automatically.
- **Right of entry (power of termination).** The grant is made *on condition*, for example "to the town, *but if* it stops being a park, the grantor may take it back". When the condition is broken, the grantor has to **act** to reclaim the property. Nothing happens automatically.

**Remainders** are future interests given to **someone other than the grantor**. They take effect when the earlier interest ends naturally:

- "To Ann for life, **then to Ben**." Ann holds a life estate, and Ben holds a **remainder**.
- A **vested remainder** goes to a known person with no conditions attached. Ben is certain to take the property, or his estate will if he dies first.
- A **contingent remainder** depends on a condition, or goes to a person not yet known, for example "then to Ben *if he has graduated college*" or "then to Ann's *first child*".
- An **executory interest** is a related interest that *cuts short* someone else's interest rather than waiting for it to end, for example "to Ann, but if she marries, to Ben".

**Why this matters in disputes:**
- The holder of a present interest may use the property but may not destroy its value. Doing so is **waste**, and the holder of the reversion or remainder can sue to stop it.
- The future-interest holder's claim appears in the chain of title. A buyer of the present interest takes only what the seller had, which is a life estate, not full ownership.
- Many disputes over land, inheritances and trusts come down to working out **who holds the next interest in line**.

**On the balance sheet, dividing ownership over time also divides its value.**

- **Ann's life estate** is worth the use of the property for the rest of her expected life.
- **Ben's remainder** is worth the property's value, discounted for the wait until it arrives.

Appraisers and the IRS split the value with actuarial tables, and the two interests always add up to the whole. As the years pass, value moves from one holder to the other. The life estate shrinks like an asset being amortized, and the remainder grows toward full ownership. When Ann dies, nothing is sold. Her interest simply ends, and Ben's asset becomes the whole property.

### Explained for a five-year-old

Grandma has a bike. She says, "Ann, you can ride it as long as you live. After that, it goes to Ben."

- Ann gets to ride the bike **now**.
- Ben has to **wait**, but the bike is promised to him. That promise is a **remainder**.
- If Grandma had said, "After Ann, the bike comes back to me," that is a **reversion**. The bike goes home to Grandma.
- Ann can ride the bike, but she can't smash it, because it still has to be a good bike when it's Ben's turn (or Grandma's).

## Depreciable and amortizable assets

Some rights wear out. Accounting records that wearing out, so the balance sheet keeps telling the truth about what an owner still holds.

- **Depreciation** spreads the cost of a **tangible** asset with a limited useful life over the years it is used. Examples are buildings, machinery, vehicles, fences, roofs and other improvements to land.
- **Amortization** does the same for **intangible** assets with a limited life. Examples are:
  - patents and copyrights
  - purchased customer lists
  - franchise and licence rights
  - a tenant's right-of-use asset
  - leasehold improvements, which are amortized over the shorter of their useful life and the lease term
- **Neither applies** to **land**, which does not wear out, or to intangibles with no fixed end, such as renewable trademarks and, for most companies, goodwill. These are instead tested for **impairment**: they are written down only when their value has clearly fallen.

**Where it sits on the balance sheet:**
- The asset is listed at its **cost**.
- Directly beneath it is a running total of **accumulated depreciation** (or **accumulated amortization**), which is subtracted from cost.
- The difference is the **book value** (or carrying value).
- Each year's charge is an expense on the income statement, and it reduces equity.

**An example.** A commercial building and its land are bought for $500,000, of which $110,000 is the land. For U.S. tax purposes, a commercial building is depreciated over 39 years (27.5 years for residential rental property). Ignoring the partial first year, that is $390,000 ÷ 39 = $10,000 a year. After ten years, the books show:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Land | $110,000 |
| Building, at cost | $390,000 |
| Less accumulated depreciation | ($100,000) |
| **Property, net** | **$400,000** |

**What depreciation does and does not mean for ownership:**

- **Depreciation measures cost used up, not rights lost.** After ten years the owner's title to the building is exactly as complete as it was on day one. Book value is not market value either. A court valuing the property in a dispute looks at **fair market value**, not the depreciation schedule.
- **The party with the benefit and the risk takes the deduction.** That is usually the owner. A tenant who pays for improvements depreciates or amortizes them, even though the landlord owns the building, and a lessee amortizes its right-of-use asset. This is the same "follow the benefit" rule as for equitable ownership.
- **When the asset is sold:**
  - The seller removes both the cost and the accumulated depreciation, and records a gain or loss equal to the price minus book value. Tax law may **recapture** past depreciation, taxing part of the gain differently.
  - The buyer records the asset at their own cost and starts a new schedule. A purchase does not hand over the seller's depreciation.
- **Many amortizable rights end by law.**
  - A U.S. patent generally runs 20 years from filing.
  - A lease ends with its term.
  - Purchased business intangibles (Section 197 intangibles) are amortized over 15 years for U.S. tax.

  When the right ends, its asset has been amortized to zero, much like a life estate that ends with the holder's life.
- **Depreciation and waste.** A life tenant may cause ordinary wear and tear, which is depreciation. They may not destroy or strip the property beyond that, because that would be **waste** against the holder of the remainder.

**One word, two meanings.** "Amortization" also means paying off a loan's principal over time, as with a mortgage. That happens on the **liability** side:
- Each payment reduces the mortgage payable.
- The lender's claim on the property shrinks.
- The owner's equity in the property grows.

This is a right moving gradually, payment by payment, from the lender back to the owner.

## Limiting others from infringing: an escalating ladder

The law expects an owner to use the **least force that will work**. Each step up the ladder needs more justification than the one before it.

1. **Define and record the right.** Record the deed, keep receipts, bills of sale, titles and registrations, keep the asset register and depreciation schedules current, survey the boundaries, and carry title insurance where it is available. A right that is clearly documented on the public record and on the owner's books is rarely challenged.
2. **Give notice.** Fences, locks, boundary markers and "No Trespassing" signs announce the right to exclude. In many places, trespass is not a crime until the person has been told, by sign or in words, that entry is forbidden. Asking someone to leave is notice.
3. **Assert the right in writing.** A demand letter or a cease-and-desist letter states the claim and creates a record that the other party was told.
4. **Use the civil courts.** The remedies include:
   - damages for trespass or nuisance
   - an injunction ordering someone to stop
   - **ejectment** or eviction to recover possession
   - **quiet title** to settle who owns land
   - **replevin** or conversion claims to recover or be paid for personal property

   While a case is pending, it appears in the notes to both sides' financial statements as a contingency. The judgment then moves the right: the asset changes hands, or one party gains a receivable and the other takes on a liability.

   Act promptly. A neglected right can be lost, for example through adverse possession or a prescriptive easement.
5. **Call law enforcement.** Criminal trespass, theft, burglary and threats are matters for the police. A report also creates an official record.
6. **Reasonable, non-deadly force.** Most jurisdictions allow reasonable, proportionate force to stop a trespass or to prevent theft. This usually applies only after a request to leave has failed, or when there is no time to make one. The force must stop when the intrusion stops. Excessive force turns the owner into the wrongdoer.
7. **Deadly force, in defence of persons in the home.** This is the last step, and the narrowest.

## Defending the home with lethal force

At this step the balance sheet stops being the measure. Property can be replaced, recovered or paid for, and **no asset on any balance sheet justifies taking a life to protect it.** What the law protects here is the people in the home.

Many legal systems give the home special protection. This is often called the **castle doctrine**: a person attacked in their own home does not have to retreat before defending themselves. The general principles common to most U.S. states are:

- **Deadly force protects people, not property.** It is generally lawful only when a person reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent **imminent death or serious bodily harm** to themselves or others in the home. In some states, preventing a violent forcible felony such as a home invasion also qualifies. Using deadly force only to stop theft, vandalism or simple trespass is generally unlawful.
- **The belief must be reasonable.** The question is whether a reasonable person in the same situation would have feared death or serious injury, not just whether the defender was afraid.
- **Presumptions in the home.** Many states presume that this fear is reasonable when someone **unlawfully and forcibly enters an occupied dwelling**. The presumption usually does not protect the defender against someone who has a right to be there, such as a co-resident, or against a police officer acting lawfully, and it ends once the intruder is fleeing or no longer a threat.
- **No duty to retreat in the home.** Under the castle doctrine, a defender inside their own home usually does not have to retreat first, even in states that require retreat elsewhere. Some states extend this to any place a person lawfully is ("stand your ground"). Others require retreat outside the home when it can be done safely.
- **No traps.** Spring guns and other unattended deadly devices are unlawful, even against a burglar. The leading case is *Katko v. Briney* (Iowa, 1971). The law allows deadly force only from a person present who judges an actual threat.
- **The justification ends when the threat ends.** Force used after the intruder has been subdued, disarmed or has fled is no longer defence.
- **Afterwards.** Call emergency services, give aid if it is safe, and expect a full investigation. Even a lawful use of deadly force can bring criminal review and civil suits.

## The principle underneath

Ownership is a bundle of rights the community agrees to recognise and enforce. Those rights move in many ways:

- by **sale**, **lease**, **mortgage** and **gift**
- by **inheritance** and **trust**
- by **time**, through reversions and remainders
- by **use**, through depreciation and amortization
- by **court order**

Every move is recorded twice: once where the right leaves and once where it arrives. The balance sheet shows where each right now sits:

- as an **asset** of the person who holds it
- as a **liability** of the person who owes it
- as **equity** for whatever is left

Owners protect their claims through **records** (title and accounting), **notice**, the **courts**, and the **police**. Force is lawful only to the degree that it is needed. The owner who documents their rights, can show where each right sits on the books, gives clear notice, and escalates no faster than the threat does keeps both the property and the protection of the law.
