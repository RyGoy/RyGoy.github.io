# Every entry has two sides

## The idea

Every event changes at least two things, so the books write every event down twice. **Debit** means the left side of an account, and **credit** means the right side. They don't mean good or bad, or in or out. In every entry the debits and credits add up to the same amount.

When something passes between two parties, each keeps their own books, and the same event appears in both, **on opposite sides**. That is why your bank statement says "credit" when you put money in: it is the *bank's* record. In the bank's books your deposit is money it owes you, and what it owes goes up on the right. In your own books, the same deposit is cash you have, which goes up on the left.

So the first habit of the lens: before you read an entry, ask **whose books is this?**

## In the lemonade stand

Mia puts $20 into the stand's cash tin. The stand's books: debit Cash $20 (it has more), credit Mia's Money $20 (it owes her stake more). Both sides went up by $20. The lesson [Start Here: Debits and Credits](https://thetenderforlaw.com/accounting/start-here/) walks through it in five minutes.

## In their words

> "everytime that a transaction goes from one party to another (such as a bank deposit), one parties debit is the others credit. For example, when SGMI does a job for $1,000 and receives payment, A/R is credited and the Bank account is Debited. On the customers end, the Bank Account is Credited and the A/P is Debited."
> — [Chris Schulte, An Article About Accounting](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/)

(A/R is *accounts receivable*, what customers owe the business. A/P is *accounts payable*, what the business owes.)

> "Now, they have all lied to you by showing you how to keep a CHECKBOOK REGISTER. You can go look and THIS IS NOT A LEDGER. … Your register is backwards...I suspect that this is so you wouldn't realize the value of Debits and Credits. These Registers are opposite of the Ledgers."
> — [Chris Schulte, An Article About Accounting](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/)

> "You are SURETY in a pre-authorized payment agreement. The BANK pays the debt with MONEY OF ACCOUNT and DEBITS your account to balance the books...they can. You're SURETY."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 3, 3 July 2013](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_iii/462493170453287_532494196786517/#entry-532563783446225)

## Check yourself

> [!question]- You deposit $100. Your bank statement shows a credit of $100. In your own books, which side does it go on, and why are they opposite?
> A debit to Cash, in your books: you have more. The bank credits it in *its* books because, to the bank, your deposit is money it owes you. One event, two sets of books, opposite sides.
