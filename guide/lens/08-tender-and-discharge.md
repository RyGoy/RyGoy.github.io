# Tender, and discharge

[The accounting lens](_index.md) · step 8 of 8 · You need first: [Trust, and the trustee](07-trust-and-the-trustee.md)

## The idea

The archive takes its name from this word. In Scott Duncan's words, to [**tender**](../terms/tender.md) "[in the context of Law](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/scott-duncan/legalese-for-idiots/)" means "[to present to PERSON, an unconditional offer, to enter into a contract](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/scott-duncan/legalese-for-idiots/)": "[Unconditionally offered, with use being proof of acceptance.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_v/462493170453287_622715734431029/#entry-622716431097626)" And "[ACCEPTING A TENDER IS CONSENT.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_iii/462493170453287_550101411692462/#entry-561864330516170)" He reads the words on a bank note through that definition: the note is an offer, and whoever uses it accepts the terms that come with it. That is the group's name: "[THIS NOTE IS LEGAL TENDER = THIS NOTE IS A TENDER FOR LAW.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_v/462493170453287_606995862669683/)"

[**Discharge**](../terms/discharge.md) is how a debt ends. Scott Duncan defines it as "[to "zero-out" a balance of an item on an account](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_iii/462493170453287_532937380075532/#entry-532943700074900)". Picture the debt as a line in the books with a balance on it, say $50 owed. Discharging it is the entry that brings that balance down to **zero**, so nothing more is owed on it. ("Check yourself", below, works through the entry.)

Who decides how a balance is brought to zero? Scott Duncan's answer runs through the word [**remittance**](../terms/remittance.md): "[Advice to a SECURED PARTY on how to DISCHARGE the debt](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_iii/462493170453287_532937380075532/#entry-532943700074900)". The secured party, the one owed, asks for that advice, and in his reading it is the [**surety**](../terms/surety.md), the responsible party from [step 5](05-surety-the-responsible-party.md), who gives it. When a member wrote that they had owed $20,000, he answered: "[A Secured Party was seeking remittance for $20,000. There is a HUGE Difference. The SURETY ADVISES. REMITTANCE IS ADVICE.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_i/462493170453287_495437137158890/#entry-544514452251158)" And only the surety can give that advice: "[Only the SURETY has the AUTHORITY to do that.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_i/462493170453287_495437137158890/#entry-544514452251158)"

Chris Schulte ties discharge back to the choice in [step 3](03-what-is-had-and-what-is-owed.md): when value comes into the books, the matching credit goes either to a liability or to equity. He takes the case of money lent to a corporation: "[The deposit of the asset means that there has to be an equal and opposite credit entry.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/#:~:text=The%20deposit%20of%20the%20asset%20means%20that%20there%20has%20to%20be%20an%20equal%20and%20opposite%20credit%20entry.)" Equity is the simple choice, he says, but it leaves nothing to discharge: "[The easiest is to credit an Equity/Capital account, but then you wouldn't discharge the monthly payment and build the corporations credit.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/#:~:text=The%20easiest%20is%20to%20credit%20an%20Equity%2FCapital%20account)" Recorded as a liability, the deposit is something owed, with a balance that payments can bring down: "[By crediting a liability account, you can discharge a monthly payment and build credit.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/#:~:text=By%20crediting%20a%20liability%20account%2C%20you%20can%20discharge%20a%20monthly%20payment%20and%20build%20credit.)"

We have fashioned the lens you must now study to see with. The International language of Law and Commerce is [**LEGALESE**](../terms/legalese.md), and "[LEGALESE IS NOT ENGLISH! It is a COPYRIGHTED LANGUAGE like JAVA or FORTRAN.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_vi/462493170453287_655243544511581/#entry-656937777675491)" It is a bastardized form of English where precision is the purpose: "[Legalese is a massive forest of redefined words.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_xii/462493170453287_888166014552665/)" If you are a native English speaker you will be unable to determine what a word means, legally, without this lens, so "[look up the LEGAL definition of the words I use, even (ESPECIALLY) if you … already think you know the meaning of the words.](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_ii/462493170453287_531460560223214/#entry-532416830127587)" "[Remember: ONLY LEGAL DEFINITIONS APPLY!](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_i/462493170453287_481209285248342/#entry-1016783841690881)"

## In their words

> "TENDER in the context of Law means, "to present to PERSON, an unconditional offer, to enter into a contract". Look at those words, then look at a bank note. THIS NOTE IS LEGAL TENDER means, "THIS NOTE IS AN UNCONDITIONAL OFFER TO ENTER INTO A CONTRACT FOR LAW"."
> — [Scott Duncan, Legalese for Idiots](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/scott-duncan/legalese-for-idiots/)

> "THIS NOTE IS LEGAL TENDER=THIS NOTE IS A TENDER FOR LAW … LEGAL TENDER is NOT MONEY, NOR is it CURRENCY. It is ATTACHED to money/currency."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 6, 24 June 2014](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_vi/462493170453287_685294971506438/)

> "USING LEGAL TENDER binds you. Using MONEY does not."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 10, 3 July 2015](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_x/462493170453287_847918695244064/#entry-849675381735062)

> "REMITTANCE - Advice to a SECURED PARTY on how to DISCHARGE the debt. DISCHARGE - to "zero-out" a balance of an item on an account."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 3, 4 July 2013](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_iii/462493170453287_532937380075532/#entry-532943700074900)

> "By crediting a liability account, you can discharge a monthly payment and build credit."
> — [Chris Schulte, An Article About Accounting](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/)

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Scott Duncan defines DISCHARGE as "to zero-out a balance of an item on an account". In the books, what does a discharge of a $50 debt look like?
> An entry that brings the debt's balance from $50 to zero: in the debtor's books, the liability is debited $50 (it goes down on the left), and the other side records what settled it.
