## The idea

Apply the two-sides habit from [step 2](02-every-entry-has-two-sides.md) to a bank note. In your books it is cash: something you have. Now ask whose *other* books it appears in. A central bank records every note it issues as a **liability**: something it owes. So the note in your pocket is one side of a debt, and its other side sits in the books of whoever signed it.

That is the sense of Scott Duncan's phrase **money is debt in transit**: money is a promise passing from hand to hand. And with a debt comes a responsible party ([surety](05-surety-the-responsible-party.md)), which is why he tells readers to look at whose names and signatures are on the money they hold.

He also separates two kinds of money: **money of exchange**, which buys goods and services, and **money of account**, which exists only as entries in books.

## In their words

> "Money is DEBT in transit. With DEBT there is SURETY, and you have it, when you have the money. Look at the money in your pocket! Notice there are names and signatures on it?"
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 15, 6 August 2016](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_xv/462493170453287_1044508052251793/)

> "Money is based on PROMISES, which means so is "law". … Your money is only as good as your law, and your law is only as good as your money."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 13, 22 February 2016](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_xiii/462493170453287_948042318565034/#entry-948089958560270)

> "Fiat Money is DEBT IN TRANSIT. If you can buy good/services with it, it's MONEY OF EXCHANGE. The rest is MONEY OF ACCOUNT. MONEY OF EXCHANGE can buy MONEY OF ACCOUNT, but MONEY OF ACCOUNT cannot buy MONEY OF EXCHANGE."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 14, 23 April 2016](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_xiv/462493170453287_981483405220925/#entry-985202784848987)

> "SO: Banks ONLY function as a CURRENCY EXCHANGE."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 3, 3 July 2013](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_iii/462493170453287_532494196786517/#entry-532550620114208)

> "Currency is no good unless other people accept it."
> — [Scott Duncan, Volume 10, 21 June 2015](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_x/462493170453287_845066405529293/)

## Check yourself

> [!question]- You hold a $20 note. Using the two-sides habit, name the two sets of books it appears in, and on which side of each.
> Yours, as cash you have (an asset, the debit side). And the books of the bank that issued it, as a note it owes (a liability, the credit side).
