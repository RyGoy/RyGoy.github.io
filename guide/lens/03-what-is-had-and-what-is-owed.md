# What is had, and what is owed

[The accounting lens](_index.md) · step 3 of 8 · You need first: [Every entry has two sides](02-every-entry-has-two-sides.md) · Next: [Title is all that is sold](04-title-is-all-that-is-sold.md)

## The idea

Everything in the books is found on the **balance sheet** under these three kinds of **accounts**:

- **Assets**: what the business *has*. They sit on the left: their balance is a debit.
- **Liabilities**: what the business *owes* to others. They sit on the right: a credit.
- **Equity** (also called capital): the claim of whoever put their own value in. Also on the right: a credit.

What the business has always equals what it owes plus the equity holders' claim. Chris Schulte's point is the one beginners miss: "[BOTH Liabilities AND Equity/Capital Accounts have a Credit balance](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/)". When value comes in, the books must record a claim against it on the right, and there are only two kinds: the business *owes* it back (a [liability](../terms/liability.md)), or it becomes someone's *stake* (equity). Which one is chosen changes who can demand what. Scott Duncan counts equity in his formula too: "[EQUITY is part of the "accounting" in "It's all SURETY and ACCOUNTING"](https://thetenderforlaw.com/vol_x/462493170453287_842656495770284/#entry-850726338296633)".

## In the lemonade stand

Mia's $20 of savings becomes equity: Mia's Money, her stake. Mom's $30 loan becomes a liability: IOU to Mom. Both came in as cash (an asset, debit). The difference is on the credit side: Mom can demand her $30 back; Mia's stake is only a claim on whatever is left once the stand has paid what it owes.

## In their words

> "You have to realize that Assets have a Debit balance, BUT BOTH Liabilities AND Equity/Capital Accounts have a Credit balance."
> — [Chris Schulte, An Article About Accounting](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/)

> "Accounting is transaction driven. If I come into possession of $1,000 cash and I DEPOSIT it into a corporation, the corporation can either owe me (liability) or account for it in a Capital account."
> — [Chris Schulte, An Article About Accounting](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/an-article-about-accounting/)

> "You sell the asset(s)to the corporation. The corporation incurs a liability and has an asset. The net transaction is zero."
> — [Chris Schulte, 'Robbing a bank'](https://thetenderforlaw.com/articles/chris-schulte/robbing-a-bank/)

## Practise

In the [flash cards](https://thetenderforlaw.com/accounting/flashcards/), the stand's books show the two sides after every answer: what the stand has, against what it owes plus Mia's stake.

## Check yourself

> [!question]- Chris Schulte deposits $1,000 in his corporation. Name the two ways the corporation can record the credit, and what each means for him.
> As a liability: the corporation owes him $1,000, which he can demand back. Or in a capital (equity) account: it is his stake, a claim on what is left after the corporation pays what it owes.
